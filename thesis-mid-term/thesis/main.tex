% ============================ 1. INTRODUCTION ========================
\section{Introduction}
Subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) forecasts are forecasts for two weeks to about two months ahead~\cite{vitart2017s2s}. In India this
range is most important during the summer monsoon (June to September, JJAS). A break in the rains or a long wet spell of a few weeks
can affect sowing, reservoirs and floods~\cite{imd2022lpa}.

In the last few years many AI weather models have come out, for example GraphCast, FuXi-S2S and
NeuralGCM~\cite{lam2023graphcast,chen2024fuxis2s,kochkov2024neuralgcm}. These models are usually tested with global
scores~\cite{rasp2024wb2}, so it is not clear how well they work over India, where the skill changes a lot with region and
season~\cite{malik2024s2s}. In my thesis I am looking at three questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Which forecast system works best over India, and does this depend on the variable, lead time, region and season?
\item Can a small regional model correct the bias and the spread of a global AI forecast?
\item Can a deep generative model give good probabilistic forecasts of rainfall and temperature together?
\end{enumerate}
So far I have worked on the first two questions. The third one is the plan for the remaining months.

% ============================ 2. DATA AND METHODS ===================
\section{Data and methods}
I compared nine forecast systems. Four are operational models from the S2S database (ECMWF, NCEP, UKMO and CMA). The other five are
AI or hybrid models (FuXi-S2S, NeuralGCM, FourCastNet~3, DLESyM-v0 and DLESyM-v1), which we ran ourselves from ERA5 initial
conditions. Each model was tested only on dates after its training period. I used 517 start dates between 2020 and 2024 (516 for
temperature) and averaged days 1--42 of each forecast into six weekly means on a 1.5$^\circ$ grid.

Rainfall was checked against IMERG and IMD gridded rainfall~\cite{huffman2023imerg,pai2014imd}, and 2\,m temperature against
ERA5~\cite{hersbach2020era5}. For the ensemble mean I used the anomaly correlation coefficient (ACC) and the RMSE. For the whole
ensemble I used the CRPS and the coverage, which is how often the observation falls inside the 90\% range of the ensemble. All scores
were computed for India as a whole and for the four IMD homogeneous regions.

\subsection{Calibrating FuXi-S2S}
FuXi-S2S does well for the monsoon, but its ensemble spread is much too small over India. In Week~1 only 15.6\% of the observed
weekly rainfall values lie inside its 90\% range, when it should be 90\%. To fix this I trained a small neural network with 42,434
parameters~\cite{raj2026adapter}. For every grid point and lead it predicts a shift $\delta$ and a spread factor $s$, which are
applied to each ensemble member after taking $u_m=\log(1+x_m)$:
\begin{equation}
q_m = u_m + \delta + (s-1)\,(u_m-\bar u),
\end{equation}
where $\bar u$ is the ensemble mean. The network was trained on FuXi-S2S reforecasts from 2002--2017 against IMD rainfall, with the
CRPS as the loss, and the years 2018--2019 were used to choose the final model.

On the 2022--2024 monsoons the CRPS goes down by 16.2\% against IMD and by 12.2\% against IMERG (Table~\ref{tab:adapter}). The
coverage improves at all leads, but it is still below 90\%. Figure~\ref{fig:adapter} shows that the forecast improves over almost all
of India, most of all over the Western Ghats and the Northeast. Up to now I have tested this only for one model and only for rainfall.

\subsection{Combining models and real-time forecasts}
I also tried to combine seven models using XGBoost~\cite{kimani2025piggycast,chen2016xgboost}. On the 2025 monsoon this raised the ACC
from 0.307 (simple average) to 0.347. However, just removing the mean bias from the simple average gave a slightly higher ACC of
0.369~\cite{raj2026piggy}. With only a few years of training data, it is hard to beat such simple methods.

The FuXi-S2S forecasts (100 members, Weeks~1--6) are also run in real time twice a week and shown on an experimental website,
\url{https://s2s.scdlds.com}.

% ============================ 4. PLAN ===============================
\section{Plan for the remaining work}
The method in Section~3.2 can only shift and stretch the ensemble that FuXi-S2S gives. Next, I want to train a generative model that
creates new rainfall and temperature fields together, given the FuXi-S2S forecast $c$. The plan is to first compress the fields with
an autoencoder~\cite{rombach2022ldm} and then train a network $v_\theta$ with flow matching~\cite{lipman2023flow,liu2023rectified}.
The network learns to move random noise $z_0$ towards the observed field $z_1$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,z_0,z_1}\big\|\,v_\theta\big((1-t)z_0+t z_1,\,t,\,c\big)-(z_1-z_0)\big\|^2 .
\end{equation}
A similar idea was recently used for S2S rainfall~\cite{shi2026generative}. The main tasks are listed in Table~\ref{tab:tasks}.
