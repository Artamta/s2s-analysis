% ============================ 1. INTRODUCTION ========================
\section{Introduction}
Subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) forecasting deals with lead times of roughly two weeks to two months~\cite{vitart2017s2s}. For
India the most useful part of this range is the summer monsoon, from June to September (JJAS). Farmers, reservoir managers and
disaster agencies all want to know whether the next few weeks will be wetter or drier than normal~\cite{imd2022lpa}, but this is
also the range where forecasts have the least skill.

Over the last two or three years several AI weather models have been released, such as GraphCast, FuXi-S2S and
NeuralGCM~\cite{lam2023graphcast,chen2024fuxis2s,kochkov2024neuralgcm}. Most of them are judged on global
scores~\cite{rasp2024wb2}. Skill over India can be quite different from the global picture, and it also changes from region to
region and season to season~\cite{malik2024s2s}, so a global ranking does not say much about which model to use here. My thesis
is built around three questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Which forecast system works best over India, and does the answer change with variable, lead time, region and season?
\item Can a small regional model correct the bias and the spread of a global AI forecast?
\item Can a generative deep learning model give useful probabilistic forecasts of rainfall and temperature together?
\end{enumerate}
The first two are mostly done and are described in this report. The third is what I will work on next.

% ============================ 2. DATA AND METHODS ===================
\section{Data and methods}
I compared nine forecast systems. Four of them are operational models taken from the S2S database (ECMWF, NCEP, UKMO and CMA).
The other five are AI or hybrid models: FuXi-S2S, NeuralGCM, FourCastNet~3, DLESyM-v0 and DLESyM-v1. I ran these from ERA5
initial conditions and used only dates after the end of each model's training period, so that no model is tested on data it has
already seen. In total there are 517 start dates from 2020 to 2024 (516 for temperature), all on Mondays and Thursdays. Days 1--42
of each forecast were averaged into six weekly means on a common 1.5$^\circ$ grid.

For rainfall I used two references, the IMERG satellite product and the IMD gridded gauge data~\cite{huffman2023imerg,pai2014imd},
because they do not always agree over India. Temperature at 2\,m was checked against ERA5~\cite{hersbach2020era5}. I used four
scores. The anomaly correlation coefficient (ACC) and the RMSE are computed on the ensemble mean, and for the ACC the anomalies are
taken from each model's own climatology at the same lead time. The CRPS and the 90\% coverage (how often the observation falls
inside the 90\% range of the ensemble) are computed on the full ensemble. Everything is reported for All-India and for the four
IMD homogeneous regions.

The data processing, model runs, evaluation and calibration work below were done by me, with guidance from my supervisor and
collaborators. Parts of it are written up in two workshop papers~\cite{raj2026bench,raj2026adapter}.

\subsection{Which model works best over India}
The MME in Table~\ref{tab:acc} is a plain average of six models in which every model gets the same weight, whatever its ensemble
size.\footnote{Rainfall: CMA, DLESyM-v0, FuXi-S2S, NCEP, NeuralGCM and UKMO. Temperature: CMA, DLESyM-v1, ECMWF, FourCastNet~3,
FuXi-S2S and UKMO.} In Week~1 it has the highest ACC for both variables and the lowest RMSE in 11 of the 12 variable--lead

archived fields change resolution at day~15, in the middle of that week, and I chose not to fill the gap with a different product.

\subsection{Calibrating FuXi-S2S}
The main weakness of FuXi-S2S over India is that its ensemble is far too narrow. In Week~1 only 15.6\% of the observed weekly
rainfall values fall inside the 90\% range of the ensemble. To correct this I trained a small neural network with 42,434
parameters~\cite{raj2026adapter}. For every grid point and lead it outputs a shift $\delta$ and a spread factor $s$, which are
applied to each member after the transform $u_m=\log(1+x_m)$:
\begin{equation}
q_m = u_m + \delta + (s-1)\,(u_m-\bar u),
\end{equation}
where $\bar u$ is the ensemble mean. The network was trained on FuXi-S2S reforecasts for 2002--2017 against IMD rainfall, with the
CRPS as the loss function, and 2018--2019 was kept aside for choosing the final model.

On the 2022--2024 monsoons, which were not used in training or model selection, the CRPS drops by 16.2\% against IMD and by 12.2\%
against IMERG (Table~\ref{tab:adapter}a). The ACC also goes up slightly, from 0.296 to 0.322. Coverage improves at every lead but is
still short of 90\%. Table~\ref{tab:adapter}b and Figure~\ref{fig:adapter} use all five monsoons from 2020 to 2024 to get more
cases. Since 2020 and 2021 were looked at during development, these should be read as showing where the method helps, not as an
independent test. The biggest gains are over the Western Ghats and the Northeast, which are also the wettest parts of the country.
So far I have only tried this with one model and one variable.

\subsection{Combining models and real-time forecasts}
I also tried a learned combination of seven models using XGBoost~\cite{kimani2025piggycast,chen2016xgboost}, trained on 2020--2023
and tested on the 2025 monsoon. It raised the ACC from 0.307 for the simple average to 0.347. When I later tried just removing the
mean bias from the simple average, however, the ACC was 0.369, slightly higher~\cite{raj2026piggy}. With only four or five years of
training data, simple corrections are hard to beat, and I now compare every new method against them.

Alongside this, the FuXi-S2S forecasts (100 members, Weeks~1--6) are run in real time twice a week and shown on an experimental
website, \url{https://s2s.scdlds.com}.

% ============================ 4. PLAN ===============================
\section{Plan for the remaining work}
The calibration network only shifts and scales the members at each grid point. It cannot create new spatial patterns, and it
handles rainfall on its own. For the second half of the thesis I want to train a generative model instead. Given a FuXi-S2S
forecast, it should produce many possible weekly rainfall and temperature maps together, so that the two variables stay consistent
with each other. I plan to use flow matching~\cite{lipman2023flow,liu2023rectified} on a compressed version of the
maps~\cite{rombach2022ldm}, following a similar recent study on S2S rainfall~\cite{shi2026generative}.

I will count this as a success if, on the 2025 monsoon, it gives a lower CRPS and a coverage closer to 90\% than the calibration
network. If it does not, extending the calibration network to temperature (Task~3) will be the main result of this part.
