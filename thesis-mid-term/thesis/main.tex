% ============================ 1. INTRODUCTION ========================
\section{Introduction}
Subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) forecasting deals with lead times of roughly two weeks to two months~\cite{vitart2017s2s}. For
India the most useful part of this range is the summer monsoon, from June to September (JJAS). Farmers, reservoir managers and
disaster agencies all want to know whether the next few weeks will be wetter or drier than normal~\cite{imd2022lpa}, but this is
also the range where forecasts have the least skill.

Over the last two or three years several AI weather models have been released, such as GraphCast, FuXi-S2S and
NeuralGCM~\cite{lam2023graphcast,chen2024fuxis2s,kochkov2024neuralgcm}. Most of them are judged on global
scores~\cite{rasp2024wb2}. Skill over India can be quite different from the global picture, and it also changes from region to
region and season to season~\cite{malik2024s2s}, so a global ranking does not say much about which model to use here.

My thesis follows one line of work. I first find out which forecast system is most skilful over India. I then look at where the
best AI model goes wrong and correct it with a small regional model, and finally I try to improve it further. This gives three
questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Which forecast system is most skilful over India, and does the answer depend on the variable, lead time, region and season?
\item What is the main weakness of the best AI model over India, and can a small regional network correct it?
\item Can the forecast be improved further by a generative model that produces rainfall and temperature together?
\end{enumerate}
The first two are largely complete and are described in this report. The third is the plan for the second half of the thesis.

% ============================ 2. DATA AND METHODS ===================
\section{Data and methods}
I compared nine forecast systems. Four of them are operational models taken from the S2S database (ECMWF, NCEP, UKMO and CMA).
The other five are AI or hybrid models: FuXi-S2S, NeuralGCM, FourCastNet~3, DLESyM-v0 and DLESyM-v1. I ran these from ERA5
initial conditions and used only dates after the end of each model's training period, so that no model is tested on data it has
already seen. In total there are 517 start dates from 2020 to 2024 (516 for temperature), all on Mondays and Thursdays. Days 1--42
of each forecast were averaged into six weekly means on a common 1.5$^\circ$ grid.

For rainfall I used two references, the IMERG satellite product and the IMD gridded gauge data~\cite{huffman2023imerg,pai2014imd},
because they do not always agree over India. Temperature at 2\,m was checked against ERA5~\cite{hersbach2020era5}. I used four
scores. The anomaly correlation coefficient (ACC) and the RMSE are computed on the ensemble mean, and for the ACC the anomalies are
taken from each model's own climatology at the same lead time. The CRPS and the coverage are computed on the full ensemble.
Coverage checks whether the ensemble spread can be trusted. If the ensemble places the weekly rainfall between two values with
90\% probability, the observed value should fall inside that range in about 90\% of cases, and coverage is the fraction of cases in
which it does. A value well below 0.90 means that the spread is too narrow and the forecast is overconfident
(Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}a). All scores are reported for All-India and for the four IMD homogeneous regions. The systems are listed
in Appendix~\ref{app:systems} and the scores are defined in Appendix~\ref{app:scores}.

The data processing, model runs, evaluation and calibration work below were done by me, with guidance from my supervisor and
collaborators. Parts of it are written up in two workshop papers~\cite{raj2026bench,raj2026adapter}.

\subsection{Correcting the spread of FuXi-S2S}
The main weakness of FuXi-S2S over India is that its ensemble is far too narrow. In Week~1 its coverage is only 0.156: the observed weekly rainfall
falls inside the 90\% range in about one case out of six, instead of nine out of ten. To correct this I trained a small convolutional network with 42,434
parameters~\cite{raj2026adapter} (Appendix~\ref{app:adapter}). For every grid point and lead it outputs a shift $\delta$ and a spread factor $s$, which are
applied to each member after the transform $u_m=\log(1+x_m)$:
\begin{equation}
q_m = u_m + \delta + (s-1)\,(u_m-\bar u),
\end{equation}
where $\bar u$ is the ensemble mean. The network was trained on FuXi-S2S reforecasts for 2002--2017 against IMD rainfall, with the
CRPS as the loss function, and 2018--2019 was kept aside for choosing the final model.

On the 2022--2024 monsoons, which were not used in training or model selection, the CRPS drops by 16.2\% against IMD (from 3.16 to
2.65\,mm\,day$^{-1}$) and by 12.2\% against IMERG. A simple statistical correction of the ensemble mean and spread gives a
smaller gain of 12.5\% against IMD. The ACC also goes up slightly, from 0.296 to 0.322. Coverage improves at every lead, most of all
in Week~1 where it goes from 0.16 to 0.69, but it is still below 0.90 (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}b). Figures~\ref{fig:coverage}b
and~\ref{fig:adapter} use all five monsoons from 2020 to 2024 to get more
cases. Since 2020 and 2021 were looked at during development, these should be read as showing where the method helps, not as an
independent test. The biggest gains are over the Western Ghats and the Northeast, which are also the wettest parts of the country.
So far I have only tried this with one model and one variable.

\subsection{Combining models and real-time forecasts}
I also tried a learned combination of seven models using XGBoost~\cite{kimani2025piggycast,chen2016xgboost}, trained on 2020--2023
and tested on the 2025 monsoon. It raised the ACC from 0.307 for the simple average to 0.347. When I later tried just removing the
mean bias from the simple average, however, the ACC was 0.369, slightly higher~\cite{raj2026piggy}. With only four or five years of
training data, simple corrections are hard to beat, and I now compare every new method against them.

Alongside this, the FuXi-S2S forecasts (100 members, Weeks~1--6) are run in real time twice a week and shown on an experimental
website, \url{https://s2s.scdlds.com}.

% ============================ 4. PLAN ===============================
\section{Plan: improving the forecast further}
In short, the comparison identified FuXi-S2S as the model to work with, the verification showed that its main problem over India is
an ensemble that is too narrow, and the calibration network removed a large part of this problem. The calibration network, however, only shifts and scales the members at each grid point. It cannot create new spatial patterns, and it
handles rainfall on its own. For the second half of the thesis I want to train a generative model instead. Given a FuXi-S2S
forecast, it should produce many possible weekly rainfall and temperature maps together, so that the two variables stay consistent
with each other. I plan to use flow matching~\cite{lipman2023flow,liu2023rectified} on a compressed version of the
maps~\cite{rombach2022ldm}, following a similar recent study on S2S rainfall~\cite{shi2026generative}.

I will count this as a success if, on the 2025 monsoon, it gives a lower CRPS and a coverage closer to 0.90 than the calibration
network. If it does not, extending the calibration network to temperature (Task~3) will be the main result of this part.

\section{Formulas}
\label{app:scores}
For grid cell $i$ with area weight $a_i$, forecast $f_i$ (ensemble mean), observation $o_i$, anomalies $f'_i,o'_i$ and
area-weighted means $\langle\cdot\rangle$:
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{ACC}&=\frac{\sum_i a_i(f'_i-\langle f'\rangle)(o'_i-\langle o'\rangle)}
{\sqrt{\sum_i a_i(f'_i-\langle f'\rangle)^2\,\sum_i a_i(o'_i-\langle o'\rangle)^2}}, &
\mathrm{RMSE}&=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_i a_i(f_i-o_i)^2}{\sum_i a_i}}, \qquad
\mathrm{Bias}=\frac{\sum_i a_i(f_i-o_i)}{\sum_i a_i}.
\end{align*}
For ensemble members $x_1,\dots,x_M$ and observation $y$ at one grid cell, with $x_{(1)}\le\dots\le x_{(M)}$ the sorted members,
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{CRPS}&=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}|x_m-y|-\frac{1}{2M^2}\sum_{m,n}|x_m-x_n|, &
\mathrm{fCRPS}&=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}|x_m-y|-\frac{1}{M(M-1)}\sum_{k=1}^{M}(2k-M-1)\,x_{(k)},
\end{align*}
\vspace{-0.8\baselineskip}
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{Coverage}&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\big[q_{0.05,j}\le y_j\le q_{0.95,j}\big], &
\text{CRPS reduction}&=100\,\frac{\mathrm{CRPS}_{\rm raw}-\mathrm{CRPS}_{\rm cal}}{\mathrm{CRPS}_{\rm raw}}\ \%,
\end{align*}
where $q_{0.05,j}$ and $q_{0.95,j}$ are the 5th and 95th percentiles of the ensemble for case $j$. The fair CRPS
(fCRPS)~\cite{ferro2014fair} corrects for ensemble size and is used to compare systems in Section~3.1; the plain CRPS is used for
the calibration work. Both are area-weighted like the RMSE. Every score is computed per start date and lead and then averaged over
start dates. Model climatologies come from each model's own ensemble mean for every lead (31-day window, leaving one year out).
Intervals in Section~3.1 come from a paired moving-block bootstrap over start dates (2,000 draws, blocks of 16).

\section{Calibration network}
\label{app:adapter}
\textit{Architecture.} Each member's $u_m$ and $u_m-\bar u$ pass through a shared 8-channel encoder; the outputs are sorted and
averaged over members, so the result does not depend on member order. This summary, the ensemble mean and spread, and seven
location and season fields enter a residual 3-D convolutional network with 24 channels. Two output layers give $\delta$ and
$\log s=2\tanh(a)$, so $e^{-2}\le s\le e^{2}$. The calibrated member is $x'_m=\exp(q_m)-1$ with $q_m$ from Eq.~(1).

\textit{Training.} 51-member FuXi-S2S reforecasts against IMD: 1,652 start dates (2002--2017) for training and 196 (2018--2019)
for choosing the checkpoint; 16 random members per mini-batch; CRPS loss; AdamW (learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, weight decay
$10^{-4}$), batch size 8, gradient clipping at 5, early stopping. Of three random seeds, the one with the lowest validation CRPS
was kept. One run takes 52--83\,s on an NVIDIA A30 GPU. Evaluation uses 50 members generated with the public FuXi-S2S model.

\textit{Moment correction (baseline).} Fitted on 2002--2017 only. For each lead, month and grid cell it shifts the mean of the log
rainfall (pulled towards the regional mean) and rescales the spread by a regional factor limited to 0.25--4.
