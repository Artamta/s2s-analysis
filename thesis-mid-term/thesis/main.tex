% ============================ 1. INTRODUCTION ========================
\section{Background and aim}
Subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) forecasts look about two weeks to two months ahead~\cite{vitart2017s2s}. In India this matters most
during the summer monsoon, from June to September (JJAS). Farmers, reservoir managers and disaster agencies want to know whether
the coming weeks will be wetter or drier than usual~\cite{imd2022lpa}. This is also the range where forecasts are weakest.

In the last few years many AI weather models have come out, for example GraphCast, FuXi-S2S and
NeuralGCM~\cite{lam2023graphcast,chen2024fuxis2s,kochkov2024neuralgcm}. They are mostly tested on global
scores~\cite{rasp2024wb2}. Over India the results can be different, and skill also changes between regions and
seasons~\cite{malik2024s2s}. A global ranking therefore does not tell us which model to use here.

The aim of my thesis is to get the best possible S2S forecast for India. The work has three parts:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Find the best model for India}, and check how this changes with variable, lead time, region and season.
\textit{(Done.)}
\item \textbf{Find its main problem and fix it} with a small regional network. \textit{(Done for rainfall.)}
\item \textbf{Improve it further} by predicting the forecast error and uncertainty better, and by adding meteorological and
physical knowledge. \textit{(Plan for the second half.)}
\end{enumerate}
This report describes parts 1 and 2 and the plan for part 3.

% ============================ 2. DATA AND METHODS ===================
\section{Data and setup}
I compared nine forecast systems (Appendix~\ref{app:systems}). Four are operational models from the S2S database: ECMWF, NCEP,
UKMO and CMA. Five are AI or hybrid models: FuXi-S2S, NeuralGCM, FourCastNet~3, DLESyM-v0 and DLESyM-v1. I ran these myself from
ERA5 starting conditions, and only for dates after their training period, so no model is tested on data it was trained on. There
are 517 start dates between 2020 and 2024 (516 for temperature), all on Mondays and Thursdays. Each 42-day forecast is turned into
six weekly averages on a common 1.5$^\circ$ grid.

Rainfall is checked against two data sets, IMERG (satellite) and IMD (rain gauges)~\cite{huffman2023imerg,pai2014imd}, because
they do not always agree over India. Temperature at 2\,m is checked against ERA5~\cite{hersbach2020era5}. I use four scores
(formulas in Appendix~\ref{app:scores}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{ACC}: how well the forecast pattern of above- and below-normal values matches what was observed (1 is perfect,
0 is no skill). Anomalies are taken from each model's own climatology.
\item \textbf{RMSE}: the typical size of the forecast error.
\item \textbf{CRPS}: the error of the whole ensemble, not only its mean. Lower is better.
\item \textbf{Coverage}: if the ensemble says rainfall will be between two values with 90\% probability, the observed value should
land in that range about 90\% of the time. Coverage is how often it really does. A value much lower than 0.90 means the forecast
is overconfident.
\end{itemize}
All scores are given for All-India and for the four IMD homogeneous regions.

I did the data processing, model runs, evaluation and calibration myself, under the supervision of Prof.\ Sandeep Kumar Juneja
and with close day-to-day guidance from Dr.\ Manmeet Singh.
Two workshop papers have come out of this work so far~\cite{raj2026bench,raj2026adapter}.

\subsection{Which model is best over India}
I first made a multi-model mean (MME) of six models, giving each model the same weight.\footnote{Rainfall: CMA, DLESyM-v0,
FuXi-S2S, NCEP, NeuralGCM and UKMO. Temperature: CMA, DLESyM-v1, ECMWF, FourCastNet~3, FuXi-S2S and UKMO.} Table~\ref{tab:acc}

Region by region, the answer changes. For monsoon rainfall in Weeks~3--6, FuXi-S2S is the best model
in 19 of the 20 region--week cases against IMERG and in 18 of 20 against IMD. For All-India temperature, ECMWF is the best single
model from Week~3. I also compared FuXi-S2S with ERPAS, the extended-range system that IMD runs, on 101 start dates in 2023 and
2024. FuXi-S2S had the higher ACC up to Week~4, but ERPAS did better in October--December.

So for monsoon rainfall beyond Week~2, FuXi-S2S is the strongest single model. It is also open and I can run it myself. For these
two reasons I picked it as the model to improve.

\subsection{Fixing the spread of FuXi-S2S}
The main problem with FuXi-S2S over India is that its ensemble is much too narrow. In Week~1 its coverage is only 0.156: the
observed rainfall falls inside the 90\% range in about one case in six, when it should be nine in ten.

To fix this I trained a small convolutional network with 42,434 parameters~\cite{raj2026adapter} (details in
Appendix~\ref{app:adapter}). For each grid point and lead week it gives a shift $\delta$ and a spread factor $s$. These are applied
to every member $x_m$ after the transform $u_m=\log(1+x_m)$:
\begin{equation}
q_m = u_m + \delta + (s-1)\,(u_m-\bar u),
\end{equation}
where $\bar u$ is the ensemble mean. In words, $\delta$ moves the whole ensemble up or down and $s$ makes it wider or narrower. The
network was trained on FuXi-S2S reforecasts for 2002--2017 against IMD rainfall, with the CRPS as the loss. I used 2018--2019 to
pick the final model.

I tested it on the 2022--2024 monsoons, which were not used for training or for picking the model. The CRPS goes down by 16.2\%
against IMD (from 3.16 to 2.65\,mm\,day$^{-1}$) and by 12.2\% against IMERG. A simpler statistical correction of the mean and spread
gives 12.5\% against IMD, so the network does better. The ACC also goes up a little, from 0.296 to 0.322. Coverage improves at
every lead, most of all in Week~1 (from 0.16 to 0.69), but it is still below 0.90.

Figure~\ref{fig:coverage} shows the same three scores week by week. It and Figure~\ref{fig:adapter} use all five monsoons
from 2020 to 2024 to have more cases. The network beats raw FuXi-S2S on all three scores at every lead week. I had already looked
at 2020 and 2021 while developing the method, so these figures show where it helps but are not a fully independent test. The
largest gains are over the Western Ghats and the Northeast, the wettest parts of India.

\subsection{Combining models, and real-time forecasts}
I also tried combining seven models with XGBoost~\cite{kimani2025piggycast,chen2016xgboost}, trained on 2020--2023 and tested on the
2025 monsoon. It raised the ACC from 0.307 for the simple average to 0.347. But just removing the mean bias from the simple average
gave 0.369, which is slightly better~\cite{raj2026piggy}. With only four or five years of training data, simple corrections are hard
to beat, so I now compare every new method against them.

I also run FuXi-S2S forecasts (100 members, Weeks~1--6) in real time twice a week and show them on a test website,
\url{https://s2s.scdlds.com}.

% ============================ 4. PLAN ===============================
\section{Plan for the rest of the thesis}
The calibration network fixed a large part of the spread problem, but three gaps remain. Coverage is still below 0.90, so the
forecast is still overconfident. The network does not know the weather situation, for example whether the monsoon is in an active
or a break spell. And it works on rainfall alone, one grid point at a time, so its output is not guaranteed to be physically
consistent. The tasks in Table~\ref{tab:tasks} address these gaps. Each new method will be tested on the 2025 monsoon, which was
not used to build the calibration network, against the calibration network, simple bias correction and
EMOS~\cite{gneiting2005emos}. The goal is a lower CRPS and a coverage closer to 0.90, without losing ACC.

\toprule

\midrule
1 & Predict errors and uncertainty & ML model that predicts how large the FuXi-S2S error will be at each grid point and lead week,
used to set the ensemble width so that coverage gets close to 0.90. \\
2 & Meteorology-aware correction & Add the large-scale monsoon state as input: MJO/BSISO phase~\cite{wheeler2004mjo}, ENSO and IOD

3 & Physics-informed training & Physical constraints in the loss~\cite{kashinath2021piml}: non-negative rainfall, heavy rain with
moisture convergence, and rainfall consistent with temperature (wet weeks are cooler). \\
4 & Generative model & Flow-matching model~\cite{lipman2023flow,liu2023rectified} on compressed maps~\cite{rombach2022ldm} that
produces many rainfall and temperature maps together from a FuXi-S2S forecast~\cite{shi2026generative}. \\
5 & Extend & Apply the best method to temperature, other models and the global Week~3--4 forecast for the AI Weather
Quest~\cite{aiwq2025}. \\
6 & Compare & Compare all methods on the 2025 monsoon using CRPS, ACC and coverage. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\section{Formulas}
\label{app:scores}
Notation: $i$ is a grid cell, $a_i$ its area weight, $f_i$ the forecast (ensemble mean), $o_i$ the observation, $f'_i$ and $o'_i$
their anomalies, and $\langle\cdot\rangle$ an area-weighted mean.
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{ACC}&=\frac{\sum_i a_i(f'_i-\langle f'\rangle)(o'_i-\langle o'\rangle)}
{\sqrt{\sum_i a_i(f'_i-\langle f'\rangle)^2\,\sum_i a_i(o'_i-\langle o'\rangle)^2}}, &
\mathrm{RMSE}&=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_i a_i(f_i-o_i)^2}{\sum_i a_i}}, \qquad
\mathrm{Bias}=\frac{\sum_i a_i(f_i-o_i)}{\sum_i a_i}.
\end{align*}
For one grid cell with ensemble members $x_1,\dots,x_M$ (sorted: $x_{(1)}\le\dots\le x_{(M)}$) and observation $y$,
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{CRPS}&=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}|x_m-y|-\frac{1}{2M^2}\sum_{m,n}|x_m-x_n|, &
\mathrm{fCRPS}&=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}|x_m-y|-\frac{1}{M(M-1)}\sum_{k=1}^{M}(2k-M-1)\,x_{(k)},
\end{align*}
\vspace{-0.8\baselineskip}
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{Coverage}&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\big[q_{0.05,j}\le y_j\le q_{0.95,j}\big], &
\text{CRPS reduction}&=100\,\frac{\mathrm{CRPS}_{\rm raw}-\mathrm{CRPS}_{\rm cal}}{\mathrm{CRPS}_{\rm raw}}\ \%,
\end{align*}
where $q_{0.05,j}$ and $q_{0.95,j}$ are the 5th and 95th percentiles of the ensemble for case $j$.

The fair CRPS (fCRPS)~\cite{ferro2014fair} removes the effect of ensemble size, so I use it to compare models with 4 and 50
members (Section~3.1). For the calibration work I use the plain CRPS. Both are area-weighted like the RMSE. Each score is computed
for every start date and lead week and then averaged over start dates. Each model's climatology comes from its own ensemble mean,
for every lead, using a 31-day window and leaving one year out at a time. The significance test in Section~3.1 uses a block bootstrap
over start dates (2,000 resamples, blocks of 16), which keeps nearby forecasts together because they are not independent.

\section{Calibration network}
\label{app:adapter}
\textit{How it works.} Each member ($u_m$ and $u_m-\bar u$) goes through the same small encoder, and the results are averaged over
members, so member order does not matter. This summary, the ensemble mean and spread, and seven location and season fields go into
a 3-D convolutional network (24 channels) that outputs $\delta$ and $\log s=2\tanh(a)$, so $e^{-2}\le s\le e^{2}$. The calibrated
member is $x'_m=\exp(q_m)-1$, with $q_m$ from Eq.~(1).

\textit{Training.} 51-member FuXi-S2S reforecasts with IMD as the truth: 1,652 start dates (2002--2017) for training and 196
(2018--2019) for picking the model. CRPS loss, AdamW (learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), batch size 8, 16
random members per batch, early stopping; best of three random seeds. One run takes about a minute on an NVIDIA A30 GPU. Testing
uses 50 members from the public FuXi-S2S model.

\textit{Simple correction (for comparison).} Fitted on 2002--2017 only. For each lead, month and grid cell it shifts the mean of the
log rainfall and rescales the spread by a regional factor between 0.25 and 4.
